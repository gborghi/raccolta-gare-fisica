Large Hadron Collider (10 points)

Before starting this problem, read the general instructions in the separate envelope.

This problem discusses the physics of the CERN particle accelerator known as the LHC (Large Hadron Collider). The CERN is the world's largest particle physics laboratory. Its primary purpose is to explore the fundamental laws of nature. Two beams of particles are accelerated to high energies, guided around the accelerator ring by a strong magnetic field, and finally made to collide with each other. Protons are not uniformly distributed along the circumference of the accelerator, but instead grouped into clusters called bunches. The particles produced in the collisions are observed using large detectors. You can find some typical LHC parameters in Table 1.

Particle physicists use convenient units for energy, momentum, and mass.
Energy is measured in electronvolts [eV]. By definition, 1 eV is the amount of energy acquired by a particle with elementary charge, e, moving through a potential difference of one volt (1 eV =
1.602 $\cdot10-19$ kg $m2s-2$).
Momentum is measured in units of eV/c, and mass is measured in units of eV/c², where c is the speed of light in vacuum. Since 1 eV is a very small amount of energy, particle physicists often use MeV (1 MeV = 10⁶ eV), GeV (1 GeV = 10⁹ eV), or TeV (1 TeV = 10¹² eV).

Part A deals with the acceleration of protons and electrons. Part B addresses particle identification in collisions at CERN.

In the following section, particles produced in a typical LHC detector are identified using a two-phase detector consisting of a tracker and a time-of-flight (ToF) detector. Figure 3 shows the apparatus in a transverse plane and in a longitudinal plane relative to the proton beams. Both detectors are tubes surrounding the interaction region, with the beam passing through the central part of the tubes. The tracker measures the trajectory of a charged particle as it passes through a magnetic field whose direction is parallel to the proton beams. The radius r of the trajectory allows determination of the particle’s transverse momentum pT. Since the instant of collision is known, the ToF detector requires only one tube to measure the flight time, which is measured from the collision point to the ToF tube. This ToF tube is located just outside the tracker. In this question, you may assume that all particles created in the collision travel perpendicular to the proton beams, i.e., the particles produced in the collision have no momentum component along the direction of the proton beams.
